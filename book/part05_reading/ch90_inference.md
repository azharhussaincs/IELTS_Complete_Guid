## Theory / Explanation

### 90.1 What "Inference" Means in IELTS

Inference questions require understanding **what is implied** but not always stated in exact words. However, IELTS inference is **conservative**:

- The implication must be **strongly supported** by textual evidence.
- Multiple interpretations exist → usually **NOT GIVEN** or wrong MCQ option.
- Outside knowledge and world logic alone → **invalid**.

**Valid inference:** Passage states "Without vaccines, outbreak models predicted catastrophic mortality." → Infer: vaccines reduced predicted mortality (if models linked).

**Invalid inference:** Passage describes vaccine success → infer "all diseases will be eradicated by 2030" (not supported).

### 90.2 Writer's Views vs Facts

| Layer | Description | Question type |
|-------|-------------|---------------|
| **Reported fact** | Data, events, cited findings | TFNG |
| **Reported opinion** | Someone else's view quoted | TFNG unless attributed |
| **Writer's view** | Authorial evaluation, recommendation | YNNG / attitude MCQ |
| **Implied writer view** | Built from hedging, contrast, selection of evidence | Inference / YNNG |

**Key skill:** Separate **voice layers** in mixed passages.

### 90.3 Attitude and Evaluation Markers

**Positive evaluation:** fortunately, remarkably, a welcome development, compelling evidence, justified

**Negative evaluation:** regrettably, questionable, oversimplified, premature, inadequate, misleading

**Neutral/reporting:** according to, studies show, data indicate (without evaluative adverb)

**Sceptical:** purported, alleged, so-called, ostensibly, it remains unclear

**Why markers matter:** YNNG often hinges on one adverb the writer chose.

### 90.4 Modality and Certainty

| Modal / phrase | Certainty level |
|----------------|-----------------|
| must, will, certainly | Strong claim |
| may, might, could | Possibility |
| often, tend to, generally | Qualified generalisation |
| appears to, seems, suggests | Cautious inference by writer |

**Statement with "will"** vs **passage "might"** → FALSE/NO for certainty mismatch.

### 90.5 Rhetorical Structure as Inference Clue

**Contrast structure:** Writer spends paragraph refuting X → likely view: anti-X.

**Concessive structure:** "While X is popular, the evidence supports Y" → writer favours Y.

**Problem-solution:** Writer emphasises solution S → implied approval of S unless critique follows.

**Selection of evidence:** Writer cites only failures of Policy A, successes of Policy B → implied preference (careful — may still be NG if writer doesn't evaluate).

### 90.6 Inference in MCQ vs YNNG

**YNNG:** Binary yes/no/not given on statement matching writer view.

**MCQ inference:** Choose best-supported implication from four options.

**MCQ strategy:** Eliminate options requiring **extra steps** of reasoning. Prefer **single-step** implication.

### 90.7 Writer vs Researcher vs Public

Passages often include:

- Research findings (fact layer)
- Researcher's interpretation (quoted opinion)
- Writer's comment on interpretation (authorial layer)

**Example:**

"Dr. Patel argued the results were definitive. **However, the sample size limits any such certainty.**"

Writer view: sceptical of definitiveness → NO to "Writer agrees results are definitive."

### 90.8 Double Negative and Understatement

"Not insignificant" = significant  
"It is uncommon for critics to praise the work" = critics rarely praise  
"Less than ideal" = negative evaluation (understatement)

Band 7+ must parse **negative polarity** accurately.

### 90.9 Inference Boundaries Table

| Supported | Unsupported |
|-----------|-------------|
| Direct entailment from stated facts | Future predictions not in text |
| Writer's evaluative language | Reader's moral judgement |
| Logical consequence explicitly framed | Statistical extrapolation beyond data |
| Paraphrase of implied conclusion | Assumption about unstated motives |

## Examples

### Example 1: Valid Inference

**Passage:** "The treaty lacks enforcement mechanisms."

**Inference MCQ:** What problem does the writer identify?

**A.** Funding shortages  
**B.** Absence of compliance powers ✓  
**C.** Public opposition  
**D.** Historical precedent  

B directly entailed.

### Example 2: YNNG Writer View

**Passage:** "Calling the initiative a success, as some ministers have, **ignores persistent regional disparities**."

**Statement:** The writer believes ministers' claims are misleading. → **YES**

### Example 3: NOT GIVEN on Inference

**Passage:** "Urban beekeeping has grown in popularity."

**Statement:** The writer encourages readers to keep bees. → **NOT GIVEN** (popularity stated; encouragement not stated)

## Worked Examples

### Worked Example 1: Layer Separation

**Passage:** "Industry spokespeople maintain that emissions targets are achievable. **Independent auditors disagree**, citing outdated infrastructure."

**Question:** Writer's view on achievability?

Writer aligns with **auditors** (disagree with industry) — targets **questionable/ not achievable** as presented.

### Worked Example 2: MCQ Inference Restraint

**Passage:** "Early trials showed modest gains, **insufficient to justify nationwide rollout**."

**Options:**

A. Trials were fraudulent  
B. Gains did not support full deployment ✓  
C. Nationwide rollout has occurred  
D. Writer opposes all research  

B is minimal supported inference. D overgeneralises.

### Worked Example 3: Hedging

**Passage:** "The correlation **may reflect** shared economic factors rather than direct causation."

**Statement:** Writer is certain causation is direct. → **NO** (may reflect = uncertainty, suggests alternative)

## Exercises / Passages

### Passage: Autonomous Vehicles and Urban Policy

Proponents of autonomous vehicles (AVs) claim they will eliminate human error, the factor implicated in most road fatalities. Municipal planners have responded with varying enthusiasm. While Singapore has designated test corridors and revised liability statutes, several European cities have imposed moratoriums until pedestrian safety data accumulate. The author acknowledges that AVs could reduce congestion **if** shared-ownership models prevail, yet warns that without regulatory intervention, privately owned AVs might increase total vehicle kilometres travelled. **It would be premature**, the author concludes, **to dismantle public transit investments on the promise of a technology still evolving**.

**YNNG — Writer's views**

1. The writer accepts that human error causes most road deaths.
2. The writer believes Singapore's approach is the only viable model.
3. The writer thinks public transport funding should be protected pending AV maturity.
4. The writer is entirely opposed to autonomous vehicle development.
5. Shared ownership could help avoid congestion increases.

**Inference MCQ**

6. The writer's overall stance toward rapid AV-driven transit replacement is  
   A. Enthusiastic support  
   B. Cautious scepticism ✓  
   C. Neutral description without view  
   D. Complete dismissal of technology  

7. European moratoriums suggest cities prioritise  
   A. economic growth over safety  
   B. safety data before expansion ✓  
   C. Singaporean regulations  
   D. increased vehicle kilometres  

### Exercise Set B (8–10)

8. "Premature" in conclusion signals writer view?
9. Inference beyond text equals?
10. Reported speech without writer tag — whose view?

---

## Solutions

**1. YES** — Writer reports proponents' claim; does not dispute fatality factor (accepts as given in argument structure). *Borderline: if strict, could argue writer reports proponents only — but "factor implicated in most" presented as background fact.*

**2. NO** — Singapore cited as example; "only viable" not stated; Europe differs.

**3. YES** — "Premature to dismantle public transit investments" = protect funding.

**4. NO** — Writer sees potential benefits (congestion reduction if shared ownership); not entire opposition.

**5. YES** — "could reduce congestion if shared-ownership models prevail."

**6. B** — Cautious scepticism: potential acknowledged, warnings and premature conclusion.

**7. B** — Moratoriums until pedestrian safety data accumulate.

**8. Negative evaluation — action not yet justified  
**9.** Invalid / usually NG or wrong MCQ  
**10.** Original speaker's, not writer's unless signalled

## Review

- IELTS inference = **conservative**, text-anchored.
- Separate **fact**, **quoted opinion**, **writer view**.
- Attitude markers and modality reveal stance.
- Contrast/concession structure signals preferred view.
- YNNG: writer endorsement required.
- MCQ inference: **minimum** supported implication.
- NOT GIVEN when writer silent on specific claim.

## Exam Tips

1. **Underline evaluative words** in passage during first read.
2. **YNNG before TFNG** on same passage if writer view — mindset shift.
3. **Quoted speech** — default not writer until writer agrees.
4. **MCQ inference** — reject "future prophecy" options.
5. **Double negatives** — rewrite in margin as positive/negative plain English.
6. **Conclusion paragraph** — writer views often crystallise there.
7. **Distinguish report from endorse** — "X claims" ≠ writer believes X.
8. **Sceptical markers** — purported, alleged — writer distancing.
9. **If two MCQ seem valid** — choose narrower, text-closer option.
10. **Review Ch 83 YNNG** — foundation for this chapter.

## Band 9 Strategies

1. **Default scepticism on inference MCQ** — prove each inference step from text.

2. **Writer view summary sentence:** after reading, write one sentence "Writer believes ___" — validate against YNNG.

3. **Modality grid:** mark must/may/might in evaluative sentences — instant certainty map.

4. **Reject psychologising** — writer's unstated motives → NG always.

5. **Rhetorical peak:** writer's strongest evaluation often in conclusion — weighted read.

6. **Contrast pivot word:** word after "but/however/yet" frequently carries writer view.

7. **Minimal inference MCQ** — Band 9 chooses boringest correct option.

8. **Cross-check TFNG/YNNG pairs** — same topic may test fact vs view separately.

9. **NG courage on attitude** — writer describes controversy without side → NG on who is right.

10. **Corpus of attitude adjectives** — maintain personal list from every mock (compelling, dubious, premature...).

### 90.10 Sarcasm and Irony (Rare)

Occasionally writers use irony: "The policy was a **masterclass in transparency**" following description of secret negotiations.

**YNNG:** Writer view opposite to literal praise → NO to "writer praises transparency."

### 90.11 Balanced vs One-Sided Passages

Some academic texts present multiple views without explicit author preference until conclusion.

**Rule:** Until writer signals alignment, statements about "writer believes view A" → **NOT GIVEN** if A and B presented neutrally.

### 90.12 Inference Question Wording

| Phrase | Demand level |
|--------|--------------|
| "The passage suggests..." | Moderate inference |
| "It can be inferred..." | Conservative inference |
| "The writer implies..." | Writer stance + inference |
| "According to the passage..." | Usually explicit — not inference |

## Additional Passage: Universal Basic Income Debates

Trials of Universal Basic Income (UBI)—unconditional cash transfers to all citizens—have multiplied from Finland to Kenya. Advocates argue UBI simplifies welfare bureaucracy and cushions automation-induced displacement. Randomised evaluations report improved mental health and modest entrepreneurial activity among recipients, though employment effects remain statistically indistinguishable from control groups in several studies. Fiscal critics project unsustainable tax burdens if UBI replaces targeted programmes without eliminating administrative savings. The author contends that **pilot scale prevents definitive verdicts**: local experiments cannot simulate macroeconomic feedback loops of nationwide implementation.

Some libertarian proponents favour UBI paired with dismantling public services—a package the author **explicitly rejects** as regressive. Progressive supporters envision UBI as supplement to healthcare and education, aligning more closely with the author's qualified endorsement **provided** funding derives from progressive taxation and carbon dividends rather than regressive consumption taxes.

**YNNG:**

1. The writer believes pilot studies conclusively prove UBI reduces employment.  
2. The writer opposes pairing UBI with elimination of public services.  
3. The writer unconditionally supports libertarian UBI models.  
4. The writer accepts UBI if funded through progressive taxation.  

**Inference MCQ:**

5. The writer's attitude toward nationwide UBI rollout is  
   A. Unqualified enthusiasm  
   B. Caution pending macro evidence ✓  
   C. Complete rejection  
   D. Indifference  

**Solutions with WHY:**

1. **NO** — Employment effects "**indistinguishable**" — not conclusive proof of reduction; writer says pilots can't give definitive verdicts.  
2. **YES** — Author "**explicitly rejects**" libertarian package dismantling services.  
3. **NO** — Rejects libertarian model.  
4. **YES** — "**Qualified endorsement provided** funding derives from progressive taxation."  
5. **B** — Pilot scale prevents definitive verdicts; macro feedback loops un simulated.

## Comprehensive Inference Reference Table

| Passage language | Likely inference | Invalid leap |
|------------------|------------------|--------------|
| "may", "might" | Possibility only | Certainty |
| "often", "tend to" | General trend | Always/never |
| "suggest", "indicate" | Tentative conclusion | Proven fact |
| "challenge", "question" | Writer scepticism | Writer endorsement |
| "unlike", "whereas" | Contrast favouring second | Equal approval |
| "premature", "hasty" | Negative evaluation | Neutral report |
| "remarkably", "notably" | Positive emphasis | No attitude |

## Full Mini-Passage 2: Algorithmic Hiring

Employers increasingly deploy algorithms screening résumés before human review. Vendors market these systems as bias-reducing because code ignores names and photographs. Independent audits, however, reveal that training data reflecting historical hiring patterns can reproduce demographic skew indirectly through proxy variables such as postcode or extracurricular keywords correlated with socioeconomic status. The author maintains that **technological neutrality claims warrant scepticism** unless accompanied by transparent validation across protected groups. Regulation in the European Union now classifies high-risk employment AI under mandatory conformity assessments—a development the author welcomes while noting enforcement capacity remains uneven across member states.

**Questions:**

A. YNNG: The writer believes algorithms are inherently unbiased. → **NO** (scepticism of neutrality claims)  
B. YNNG: The writer supports EU regulatory classification. → **YES** (welcomes development)  
C. TFNG: All EU states enforce AI rules equally. → **NG** (enforcement uneven — not equal, but "all equally" not precisely contradicted if some enforce — actually "uneven" contradicts equal enforcement → **FALSE**)  
D. MCQ: Proxy variables matter because they — **B. carry hidden demographic correlations** ✓
