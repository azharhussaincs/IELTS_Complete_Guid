## Theory / Explanation

### 114.1 What IELTS Speaking Measures

IELTS Speaking is a **face-to-face oral interview** with a certified examiner. It lasts **11–14 minutes** and assesses your ability to communicate in English across familiar and abstract topics. You are not tested on factual knowledge of IELTS topics; you are tested on whether you can:

1. **Express opinions and information** clearly and coherently.
2. **Develop answers** beyond one-word or memorised responses.
3. **Use a range of vocabulary and grammar** appropriate to the context.
4. **Be understood** without excessive listener effort.
5. **Handle follow-up questions** and more abstract discussion in Part 3.

The same Speaking test format is used for **Academic** and **General Training** candidates. Your Speaking score contributes equally to both versions.

### 114.2 Test Structure at a Glance

| Feature | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| Format | One-to-one interview with examiner |
| Duration | 11–14 minutes total |
| Parts | 3 (Introduction → Long turn → Discussion) |
| Recording | Audio recorded for quality monitoring |
| Scoring | Four criteria, each 25% of final band |
| Result | Single overall band (half-band increments) |

**The three-part architecture:**

| Part | Duration | Format | Purpose |
|------|----------|--------|---------|
| Part 1 | 4–5 min | Short questions on familiar topics | Warm-up; basic fluency and coherence |
| Part 2 | 3–4 min | 1-min prep + 1–2 min monologue on cue card | Sustained speech; organisation |
| Part 3 | 4–5 min | Two-way discussion linked to Part 2 theme | Abstract ideas; justification and speculation |

**Why the structure matters:** Part 1 feels conversational but is still assessed. Part 2 is the only time you speak uninterrupted at length — organisation here strongly signals Band 7+. Part 3 separates candidates who can only describe personal experience from those who can analyse and evaluate ideas.

### 114.3 The Four Assessment Criteria

Your final band is **not** an average of four separate scores visible to you; the examiner produces **one holistic band** informed by four equal-weight criteria:

#### Fluency and Coherence (FC)

- Speaks at length without unnatural pauses
- Uses connectives and discourse markers appropriately
- Develops topics logically; easy to follow

#### Lexical Resource (LR)

- Uses vocabulary flexibly and precisely
- Paraphrases effectively when needed
- Uses less common items with awareness of collocation and style

#### Grammatical Range and Accuracy (GRA)

- Produces a mix of simple and complex structures
- Maintains control; errors are rare and do not impede communication

#### Pronunciation (P)

- Individual sounds, word stress, sentence stress, and intonation are clear
- Can be understood throughout; accent is not penalised if intelligible

**Critical insight:** Weakness in one criterion **caps** your overall performance. A candidate with Band 9 vocabulary but Band 5 fluency (long freezes, reformulating every sentence) will not receive Band 8 overall. Examiners balance criteria; they do not reward isolated strengths.

### 114.4 How Bands Are Assigned

Examiners use **public band descriptors** (see Part 1, Chapter 3). They do not compare you to other candidates on your test day; they compare your performance to descriptor statements.

**Observable differences:**

| Band | Typical performance |
|------|---------------------|
| 5 | Short answers; frequent repetition; limited vocabulary; errors cause some strain |
| 6 | Willing to speak at length but coherence may break; adequate vocabulary; mixed grammar control |
| 7 | Speaks at length with occasional hesitation; uses less common vocabulary; complex structures with some errors |
| 8 | Fluent with only occasional repetition; wide vocabulary; majority of sentences error-free |
| 9 | Fully fluent; precise vocabulary; flexible grammar; effortless intelligibility |

**Half bands:** Examiners may award 6.5, 7.5, etc., when performance sits between descriptor levels.

### 114.5 Part 1: Introduction and Interview

The examiner verifies your identity, then asks **4–12 questions** across **2–3 familiar topic areas** (home, work/study, hobbies, daily routines, transport, etc.).

**Characteristics:**
- Questions are closed and open: *"Do you work or study?"* → *"What do you enjoy about your job?"*
- Answers typically need **2–4 sentences**, not essays.
- Follow-up questions probe the same topic before switching.

**Strategic implication:** Part 1 sets the tone. One-word answers signal low band; over-long monologues waste time and feel unnatural.

### 114.6 Part 2: Individual Long Turn

You receive a **cue card** with a topic and bullet points. You have **1 minute to prepare** (paper and pencil provided) and must speak for **1–2 minutes**. The examiner stops you at 2 minutes.

**Typical cue card structure:**
- Main prompt: *"Describe a place you visited that impressed you."*
- Bullet points: where / when / who with / why it impressed you
- Optional final line: *"And explain whether you would recommend it."*

After your long turn, the examiner asks **1–2 brief follow-up questions** (rounding-off questions).

**Strategic implication:** Notes during preparation are essential. A clear structure (past → detail → opinion) prevents mid-speech panic.

### 114.7 Part 3: Two-Way Discussion

Part 3 questions **extend the Part 2 theme** to society, trends, comparisons, and hypotheticals. Questions become more abstract: *"Why do people enjoy travelling to historical sites?"* → *"Should governments charge higher fees for tourist attractions?"*

**Characteristics:**
- Answers require **4–6 sentences** with explanation and examples.
- Examiners may challenge or redirect: *"Why do you think that?"*
- Speculation and balanced views are valued at higher bands.

**Strategic implication:** Part 3 rewards the same skills as Writing Task 2 — developing ideas, qualifying claims, using examples — but **orally and under time pressure**.

### 114.8 What Examiners Are Not Assessing

Understanding what **does not** affect your score prevents wasted effort:

| Not assessed | Why it matters |
|--------------|----------------|
| Your accent origin | Intelligibility matters; native-like accent does not |
| Whether the examiner agrees with you | No "correct" opinion on abstract topics |
| Body language (mostly) | Assessed on speech; minimal eye contact alone won't fail you |
| Factual accuracy of personal stories | Invent plausible examples if needed |
| British vs American vocabulary | Both acceptable if natural and accurate |

**What *is* penalised:** Memorised scripts detected by unnatural delivery; answers that ignore the question; silence beyond brief thinking pauses.

### 114.9 Test-Day Procedure

1. **Check-in:** ID verification; belongings stored per centre rules.
2. **Waiting:** You may speak to other candidates; stay calm.
3. **Entry:** Examiner greets you; recording begins.
4. **ID check:** Name, candidate number, nationality confirmation.
5. **Parts 1–3:** Continuous interview; no breaks between parts.
6. **End:** Examiner thanks you; you leave. No instant score.

**Recording:** All tests are recorded. A senior examiner may re-mark a sample for quality assurance. This protects **you** as well as standardises marking.

### 114.10 Computer-Delivered vs Paper-Based Speaking

Speaking is **always face-to-face** with a human examiner — even when Listening, Reading, and Writing are computer-delivered. Some centres offer **video-call Speaking** (IELTS Online) with the same format and criteria.

**Implication:** Practice with real humans, not only AI apps. Apps help with drills; they cannot replicate examiner follow-ups or Part 3 pressure.

### 114.11 Common Failure Patterns

| Pattern | Symptom | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Part 1 minimalism | "Yes." / "No." / "Sometimes." | Extend with reason + example rule |
| Part 2 under-length | Stops at 45 seconds | Structured notes; practise timing |
| Part 2 over-memorisation | Robotic delivery; wrong topic fit | Flexible templates, not scripts |
| Part 3 essay mode | 90-second answers; examiner interrupts | Answer directly, then develop |
| Vocabulary panic | Long pauses searching for "Band 9 words" | Paraphrase simply; upgrade after |
| Grammar freeze | Only short simple sentences | Pre-practise complex patterns safely |
| Pronunciation rush | Mumbling to hide errors | Slow down; stress key words |

### 114.12 Integration with Other Modules

Speaking connects directly to:

- **Listening (Part 4):** Accent exposure, signpost language, natural rhythm.
- **Vocabulary (Part 3):** Topic lexis (Chapters 45–70) feeds Part 3 depth.
- **Grammar (Part 2):** Tense control, conditionals, relative clauses for complex answers.
- **Writing Task 2 (Part 7):** Idea development, paragraph logic transfers to Part 3.

**Weekly integration drill:** After each Speaking mock, extract 10 useful phrases into your vocabulary notebook and recycle them in Writing.

### 114.13 Building a Speaking Study Cycle

**Phase A — Component drills (daily 15–20 min):**
- Pronunciation focus (Chapter 121)
- Fluency extension (Chapter 122)
- Part 1/2/3 strategy chapters (115, 117, 119)

**Phase B — Timed simulation (2–3× weekly):**
- Full mock with partner or tutor
- Record and self-assess against four criteria

**Phase C — Exam simulation:**
- Unfamiliar examiner/partner
- Strict timing including 1-minute Part 2 prep
- Post-mortem error log by criterion

## Examples

### Example 1: Part 1 Question Ladder

**Examiner:** "Do you live in a house or an apartment?"  
**Weak:** "Apartment."  
**Strong:** "I live in a rented apartment in the city centre — it's a two-bedroom flat on the fourth floor, which suits me because I'm close to work."

**Examiner (follow-up):** "What's your favourite room?"  
**Strong:** "Probably the balcony, actually. It's small, but I keep plants there, and it's where I drink coffee before starting work."

### Example 2: Part 2 Cue Card (Extract)

> **Describe a skill you learned that you found useful.**  
> You should say:  
> - what the skill was  
> - when and how you learned it  
> - who helped you  
> and explain why it has been useful for you.

**Note structure (1 minute):**
- Skill: basic coding (Python)
- When: university, second year, online course
- How: weekly projects; friend mentor
- Useful: automates spreadsheet tasks at work

### Example 3: Part 3 Escalation

**Part 2 theme:** A useful skill.

**Part 3 questions (increasing abstraction):**
1. "What skills do you think children should learn at school?" (general)
2. "Are practical skills more important than academic subjects?" (comparison)
3. "How might technology change the skills people need in the future?" (speculation)

## Worked Examples

### Worked Example 1: Diagnosing a Band 6.0 Plateau

**Profile:** Candidate feels "fluent" in daily life; mock score 6.0; target 7.0.

**Mock observations:**
- Part 1: Good length but repetitive *"I think… I think…"*
- Part 2: 1 minute 10 seconds; lost thread after example
- Part 3: Ideas present but no examples; grammar errors on third conditional

**Diagnosis:** Fluency/Coherence and GRA limit the score; LR is adequate.

**Plan:** Chapter 122 fluency drills; Part 2 timing practice 3× weekly; conditional review (Chapter 26); record and count discourse marker variety.

### Worked Example 2: Criteria Trade-Off

**Scenario:** Candidate uses advanced vocabulary (*" ramifications," " ubiquitous"*) but mispronounces them and pauses 3 seconds before each.

**Examiner perspective:** LR attempt is noted, but FC and P suffer; overall band likely **6.0–6.5**, not 7.5.

**Fix:** Master pronunciation of words you use (Chapter 121); prefer simpler accurate lexis under pressure.

### Worked Example 3: Part 2 Timing Map

**Target:** 2 minutes ≈ 240–280 words at natural pace.

| Segment | Time | Content |
|---------|------|---------|
| Opening | 0:00–0:20 | State topic + context |
| Body point 1 | 0:20–0:50 | First bullet with detail |
| Body point 2 | 0:50–1:20 | Second bullet + sensory detail |
| Body point 3 | 1:20–1:45 | Third bullet / who helped |
| Closing | 1:45–2:00 | Why useful + recommendation |

## Solutions (with WHY)

### Set A

1. **Three** — standard format worldwide.
2. **One minute** — use fully for notes, not silent memorisation.
3. **One to two minutes** — under 1 minute limits FC at higher bands.
4. **Fluency and Coherence, Lexical Resource, Grammatical Range and Accuracy, Pronunciation.**
5. **No** — identical Speaking test.
6. **Recording starts** — for monitoring; know this to reduce surprise.
7. **Part 2** — individual long turn.
8. **No** — unless intelligibility suffers.
9. **Brief question after Part 2** — e.g., "Would you visit again?"
10. **11–14 minutes** — varies by examiner pacing.
11. **Fluency and Coherence** — linking and flow.
12. **Yes, once** — paraphrase request allowed; not repeated help.
13. **Paper, pencil, cue card** — notes are not collected for marking.
14. **No** — one holistic band from whole interview.
15. **No direct penalty** — but offer alternative angle; silence hurts FC.

### Set B

16. **FC** — hesitation/repetition.  
17. **GRA** — morphology error.  
18. **P** — word stress.  
19. **LR** — limited range/repetition.  
20. **FC** — coherence breakdown.  
21. **GRA** — complex structure (positive).  
22. **P** (possibly FC) — intelligibility primary.

### Set C

23. **Part 2** — cue card long turn.  
24. **Part 1** — personal preference.  
25. **Part 3** — abstract/general.  
26. **Part 1** — personal experience.  
27. **Part 3** — policy/speculation.  
28. **Part 2** — cue card.

### Set D (sample extensions)

29. **Sample:** "Yes, I really enjoy cooking — especially on weekends when I'm not rushed. I usually make simple Mediterranean dishes because they use fresh ingredients and don't take too long. Last Sunday I tried a new pasta recipe I'd seen online."

**WHY 3 sentences:** Direct answer + development + specific example = Band 6+ Part 1 pattern.

## Review

- Speaking = **11–14 minutes**, **3 parts**, **one holistic band**.
- Four criteria **equally weighted** — weakest area caps performance.
- Part 1: extend naturally; Part 2: structure + timing; Part 3: develop and qualify.
- Recording is standard; examiner is not your enemy.
- Memorised scripts backfire; flexible strategies win.
- Integrate vocabulary and grammar from earlier book parts.

## Exam Tips

> **Exam Strategy:** Treat Part 1 as **real conversation** — smile, listen, answer the question asked, not a different prepared speech.

> **Exam Strategy:** In Part 2, the examiner **will** stop you at 2 minutes — hitting the full minute range is your responsibility; practise with a timer.

> **Exam Strategy:** If you misunderstand a question, ask politely: *"Could you explain what you mean by…?"* — once only.

> **Exam Strategy:** **Slow, clear speech** beats fast, mumbled speech. Fluency is not speed.

## Band 9 Tips

> **Band 9 Secret:** Band 9 speakers **listen actively** — they reference the examiner's exact wording in Part 3 (*"You asked about whether governments should…"*) showing precise engagement.

> **Band 9 Secret:** High-band candidates treat Part 2 notes as **a map, not a script** — they glance, then speak to the examiner, not the paper.

> **Band 9 Secret:** Elite performers **self-correct lightly** (*"…or rather, what I mean is…"*) without apologising or collapsing fluency.
