## Theory / Explanation

### 119.1 The Purpose of Part 3

Part 3 lasts **4–5 minutes** and is a **two-way discussion** linked to the Part 2 theme. Questions move from **personal** to **general**, **abstract**, and **analytical**.

**Example progression (Part 2: a useful skill):**
- Part 3: *"What skills are important for children today?"*
- Then: *"Should schools focus more on practical skills?"*
- Then: *"How might automation change the job market?"*

**What Part 3 tests:**
- Ability to **analyse**, not only describe
- **Fluency** under conceptual pressure
- **Lexical resource** for abstract topics (society, technology, education, environment)
- **Grammar range** — conditionals, passive, complex noun phrases

Part 3 separates **Band 6.5** from **Band 7.5+** more often than Part 1.

### 119.2 Question Type Taxonomy

| Type | Signal words | Response strategy |
|------|--------------|-------------------|
| Opinion | "Do you think…?" | Clear view + reasons |
| Compare | "Compare… / differences…" | Both sides + contrast language |
| Cause/effect | "Why…? / What causes…?" | 2–3 factors + example |
| Speculation | "How might… in future?" | Modal verbs + cautious claims |
| Evaluate | "Is it positive or negative?" | Balanced view + judgement |
| Hypothetical | "If… what would…?" | Second conditional accurately |
| Generalisation | "Why do people…?" | Avoid over-generalising; hedge |

### 119.3 The OEE Framework

**Opinion–Explain–Example** — Part 3's core structure:

1. **Opinion** — Direct answer in sentence 1.
2. **Explain** — Reasons, mechanisms, comparison.
3. **Example** — Society-level or personal illustration.

**Extended OEE+ (Band 7.5+):**
4. **Qualify** — *"That said… / On the other hand…"*
5. **Mini-conclusion** — *"So overall…"*

**Target length:** **4–6 sentences**, **40–60 seconds**. Longer answers risk examiner interruption — not always bad, but can signal lack of focus.

### 119.4 Balanced and Two-Part Answers

Many Part 3 questions invite **balance**:

**Question:** "Is tourism always beneficial for local communities?"

**Structure:**
- Sentence 1: *"Not always — it depends on how it's managed."*
- Sentences 2–3: Benefits (jobs, infrastructure)
- Sentences 4–5: Drawbacks (overcrowding, price inflation)
- Sentence 6: Judgement (*"On balance, regulated tourism helps if…"*)

**Why balance works:** Shows LR (contrast lexis) and FC (logical sequencing) without needing "correct" policy expertise.

### 119.5 Hedging and Qualification

Native-like academic speech **hedges** claims:

| Too absolute | Hedged |
|--------------|--------|
| "All teenagers use phones." | "Many teenagers seem to rely on phones." |
| "Technology ruins education." | "Technology can distract students if unused thoughtfully." |
| "Governments must ban cars." | "Governments could incentivise public transport instead." |

**Useful phrases:**
- *"It tends to… / It's often argued that…"*
- *"From what I've observed… / In many cases…"*
- *"I'm not an expert, but…"* (once only — don't undermine every answer)

### 119.6 Grammar Range for Part 3

**High-value structures:**

| Structure | Example fragment |
|-----------|------------------|
| Second conditional | *"If cities invested more in cycling, congestion might ease."* |
| Third conditional | *"If governments had acted earlier, emissions could have fallen."* |
| Passive | *"Children are often exposed to advertising online."* |
| Relative clauses | *"Policies that prioritise renewable energy…"*
| Concession | *"Although online learning is flexible, it lacks face-to-face interaction."* |
| Comparative | *"The more accessible education becomes, the more competitive the job market may get."* |

**Rule:** One or two complex structures **per answer**, used accurately.

### 119.7 Lexical Resource for Abstract Topics

Part 3 rewards **topic vocabulary** from Part 3 of this book (Chapters 45–70):

| Topic | Sample lexis |
|-------|--------------|
| Education | curriculum, literacy, vocational training, rote learning |
| Technology | automation, digital divide, privacy, algorithms |
| Environment | carbon footprint, sustainable, emissions, conservation |
| Society | demographic, inequality, urbanisation, social cohesion |
| Work | gig economy, job security, upskilling, remote work |

**Paraphrase the question** to show range: *"Why do people move to cities?"* → *"urban migration / seek better prospects / rural decline"*

### 119.8 Handling Examiner Prompts

Examiners may:
- **Rephrase** if you misunderstand
- **Ask "Why?"** to probe depth
- **Interrupt** to move on — normal, not a failure signal
- **Challenge**: *"But some people would disagree — why?"*

**Responses:**
- Welcome probes: *"That's a fair point — I think the reason is…"*
- Don't restart from zero; **extend** previous idea
- If lost: *"Could I think about that for a moment?"* — brief pause OK

### 119.9 Connecting to Part 2 Without Repeating

You **may** reference Part 2 briefly:
- *"Like the skill I mentioned earlier, communication matters in teams too."*

**Avoid:**
- Retelling entire Part 2 story
- Forcing irrelevant personal anecdotes into abstract questions

**Ratio:** ~70% general/societal, ~30% personal illustration in Part 3.

### 119.10 Common Part 3 Failure Patterns

| Pattern | Problem | Fix |
|---------|---------|-----|
| Essay mode | 2-minute monologue | OEE; stop at 6 sentences |
| Echoing question | Repeats examiner words only | Paraphrase then answer |
| No position | "It depends" without development | Depends **because** X and Y |
| Memorised statistics | Sounds fake | Use plausible examples |
| Part 1 register | Too casual for abstract topic | Slightly more formal lexis |
| Panic vocabulary | Long pauses | Simplify; paraphrase |

### 119.11 Part 3 vs Writing Task 2

| Skill | Writing Task 2 | Part 3 |
|-------|----------------|--------|
| Idea development | Paragraphs | 4–6 sentences |
| Planning time | 5 minutes | ~2 seconds |
| Evidence | Examples required | Examples helpful |
| Cohesion | Paragraph linking | Spoken markers |
| Revision | Can edit | Self-correct only |

**Transfer:** If you can outline Task 2 essays, you already have Part 3 **ideas** — practise **oral compression**.

### 119.12 Topic Preparation Strategy

Prepare **idea clusters**, not answers:

For each common theme, prepare:
- 2 advantages, 2 disadvantages
- 1 cause, 1 effect
- 1 future prediction
- 1 personal mini-example

**Themes:** education, work, technology, environment, media, health, transport, culture, family, cities.

## Examples

### Example 1: Opinion Question

**Q:** "Do you think children should learn a foreign language at primary school?"

**OEE answer:** "Yes — I think starting early is generally beneficial because young children pick up pronunciation more naturally. Schools can integrate languages through songs and games rather than heavy grammar. For example, in my country some primary schools introduce English twice a week, and pupils seem less self-conscious than teenagers. That said, it shouldn't crowd out the mother tongue — balance matters."

### Example 2: Speculation Question

**Q:** "How might cities change in the next twenty years?"

**Answer:** "I imagine they'll become more compact and public-transport oriented, partly because of climate targets. We might see fewer private cars in centres, with more cycling infrastructure and electric buses. At the same time, remote work could reduce rush-hour pressure, though housing affordability will remain a challenge. It's hard to predict exactly, but sustainability will probably drive most planning decisions."

### Example 3: Examiner Challenge

**Q:** "But isn't tourism harmful to the environment?"  
**Response:** "Absolutely — it can be, especially when visitors fly long distances for short trips. That's why many destinations now promote longer stays and off-season travel to spread impact. The harm isn't inevitable; it depends on policy and tourist behaviour."

## Worked Examples

### Worked Example 1: Band 6 → Band 7 Upgrade

**Q:** "Why do some people dislike reading?"

**Band 6:** "Because reading is boring and they prefer phones. Phones have videos. Reading takes time."

**Band 7 revision:**
- Merge choppy sentences
- Add hedge + second reason
- Include societal example

**Target:** "I think it's often a habit issue — people are used to fast digital content, so books feel slow by comparison. There's also a confidence factor; if someone struggled with reading at school, they may avoid it as an adult. In many countries libraries try to address this with community programmes, but screen time keeps rising."

### Worked Example 2: Two-Part Question

**Q:** "What problems do fast-growing cities face, and how can governments respond?"

**Plan (mental 5 sec):**
- Problems: housing, transport, pollution
- Responses: planning, investment, regulation

**Answer structure:** Problem 1 + Problem 2 + Response 1 + Response 2 + brief balance.

### Worked Example 3: Conditional Accuracy

**Q:** "If you could change one thing about education in your country, what would it be?"

**Weak:** *"If I will change, I change teacher pay."*

**Strong:** *"If I could change one thing, I'd raise teacher training standards rather than only salaries — better-trained teachers tend to improve outcomes across subjects. I'd model it on countries where mentoring is mandatory in the first two years."*

## Review

- Part 3 = **abstract discussion**, 4–5 minutes, linked to Part 2 theme.
- Use **OEE** (+ qualify for higher bands).
- **4–6 sentences** per answer; avoid essay mode.
- Hedge generalisations; use topic vocabulary accurately.
- Conditionals, concession, passive — **one per answer** if accurate.
- Interruptions and **Why?** probes are normal — extend, don't panic.

## Exam Tips

> **Exam Strategy:** **Answer first sentence** — don't build suspense; examiners assess clarity early.

> **Exam Strategy:** If you need thinking time, use a **short filler**: *"That's an interesting question — I'd say…"* — not repeated every turn.

> **Exam Strategy:** **Paraphrase** the question in your opening to show LR: *"Why people migrate to cities… I'd point to economic opportunity first."*

> **Exam Strategy:** End gracefully when you've developed — **don't wait** for the examiner to cut you off every time.

## Band 9 Tips

> **Band 9 Secret:** Band 9 candidates **anticipate the angle** of the next question — their Part 3 answers naturally set up follow-ups without scripting.

> **Band 9 Secret:** They use **precise collocation** (*"ease congestion," "bridge the digital divide"*) without sounding like a textbook.

> **Band 9 Secret:** When challenged, they **concede partial truth** then refine their position — hallmark of sophisticated discussion.

### Band 9 Model — Part 3 Mini-Set (original; theme: skills/learning)

**Q:** "What skills should schools prioritise today?"  
**A:** "I'd prioritise critical thinking and digital literacy alongside literacy and numeracy — not instead of them. Students need to evaluate online information because so much learning happens on screens now. Collaboration matters too, since many workplaces are project-based. That said, schools shouldn't neglect practical life skills like financial basics, which parents sometimes assume children will pick up naturally."

**Q:** "Has the internet made traditional teachers less important?"  
**A:** "Not really — it's changed their role. Teachers act more as guides who structure discussion rather than sole sources of facts, because facts are searchable. The human element — motivation, feedback, pastoral care — is still hard to automate. Where internet access is uneven, though, relying on online resources can widen inequality, so policy has to catch up."

**WHY Band 9:** Clear positions, balanced qualification, abstract vocabulary, natural fluency, no memorisation smell.
